Why the twilight sky turns purple

Press Next (or →) to move the Sun below the horizon; Back undoes it, and the slider scrubs anywhere. The slice shows the atmosphere above one observer; the strip at the top right is the sky they see when they look west.

Sunset

For the observer on top, the Sun sits on the horizon. Every sunbeam reaches them, and all the air overhead is lit.

Earth's shadow rises

The Sun drops below the horizon. The lowest beam that clears the ground now passes above the observer, and everything under it is in shadow. At 2° the shadow is about 4 km deep overhead.

Where the 4 km comes fromShadow height overhead = R(sec θ − 1), with R = 6371 km (mean Earth radius) and θ the Sun's angle below the horizon. Refraction is ignored.

Purple light

The beams that still reach the sky overhead skimmed low, dense air on the way, which scattered their blue out, so they arrive red. Fine particles high up scatter that red light back down. Blue scattered light fills the sky around it, and red plus blue reads as purple. The shadow keeps climbing. What happens to the purple once it passes the particles?

The purple fades

By 6° the shadow overhead is about 35 km deep, above the particle layer. No red light reaches the particles any more, and the purple goes. 6° is also where civil twilight ends.

Why the sky overhead stays deep blue

Ozone, 15–35 km up, absorbs yellow and orange light. The peaks are at 575 and 603 nm. A twilight beam crosses a long stretch of the ozone layer, so this absorption only shows at dawn and dusk: the blue hour.

End of nautical twilight

At 12° the shadow overhead is about 140 km deep, above the whole slice. The last bright arch in the west goes out, and the stars take over.

After a volcano

Back at 4°, add three times as many high particles. More of them catch the red beams, and the purple gets stronger. After the 1883 eruption of Krakatoa, red twilights were seen around the world.

Terms and assumptions

Civil twilight: the Sun's centre is 0–6° below the horizon. Nautical twilight: 6–12°. Purple light: the purple glow of civil twilight, mostly with the Sun 2–6° down. Chappuis absorption: ozone absorbing visible light, with peaks at 575 and 603 nm.

Assumptions: heights are stretched 12×, so straight sunbeams are drawn as curves. The fine-particle layer at about 22 km is placed for illustration. Colours come from a toy scattering model that follows the published explanation; it is not a radiative-transfer simulation. Refraction is ignored.
